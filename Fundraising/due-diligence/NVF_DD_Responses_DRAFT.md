## If the company could only prioritize two programs, which targets would be the most interesting ones to prioritize? What criteria would the team use to make that decision?

Sego is committed to advancing all five internal programs through initial screening, but the question is a useful intellectual exercise for articulating our prioritization framework. Within 3 months, Sego will have initial screening data on at least 4 of 5 targets (ACP3, KLK2, CEACAM5, MUC1-C; CLDN1 may lag due to transmembrane protein handling requirements), and we expect that data to sharpen prioritization naturally through our stage-gate process rather than requiring a premature portfolio decision. If the data forced a two-program choice, the criteria would be: (i) binder affinity and selectivity from initial screening, (ii) whole-molecule conjugate performance (ligand + linker + chelator), (iii) clinical positioning and differentiation versus programs already in or near the clinic, and (iv) portfolio balance across indications. On (iv), Sego would likely advance one prostate program (ACP3 or KLK2) and one non-prostate program (CEACAM5, CLDN1, or MUC1-C) to diversify indication risk and maximize the breadth of the clinical data package for Series A. ACP3 and KLK2 currently have the fastest path to hit identification given existing chemical matter, but equivalent screening data from a non-prostate target could shift the calculus. Go/no-go criteria at each transition (hit ID, cold optimization, hot optimization) are designed to surface the strongest programs on data, and the seed financing is structured to let that process run before narrowing the portfolio.

## Would that change the budget considerations and the progress of the programs? For example, would the company be able to advance even one program into the clinic and achieve Ph1 data on the $80 seed financing?

Narrowing the portfolio would extend runway per program, but it would not change our conviction that Phase 0 human dosimetry is the primary value inflection point in RLT, and the seed financing is sized to reach that milestone on multiple programs. In this modality, the gap between preclinical promise and clinical reality is wider than in small-molecule or biologics development: mouse dosimetry is a poor predictor of human pharmacokinetics, and the field has multiple examples of programs that looked compelling in animals and failed to translate. Phase 0 microdosing closes that gap directly, generating human biodistribution and organ-at-risk data that is decision-useful for investors, partners, and regulators. The recent transaction history confirms this: RayzeBio was acquired for $4.1B and Fusion for $2.4B, both on Phase 1 data that was itself built on Phase 0 dosimetry foundations. Achieving Phase 1 efficacy data within a seed round is possible in a two-program scenario, but it compresses the timeline and concentrates risk on a single readout. Sego's plan prioritizes generating Phase 0 data across two targets, selecting lead compounds with human evidence, and entering the Series A with the most de-risked and transactable dataset rather than a single early-stage efficacy signal. A multi-program Phase 0 approach adds only ~30% incremental cost per program versus a single candidate, and the breadth of human data strengthens the Series A negotiating position materially.

## For prostate cancer, what subset of patients would be best suited for a KLK2 or ACP3-targeted therapy especially when considering the dominant PSMA-targeted approaches? Would this be post-ARPI, PSMA negative or refractory patients for example? Is this pre or post Pluvicto? How would this generally compete with PSMA RLTs – not just Pluvicto but also next-gen PSMA approaches?

The initial patient population for both ACP3 and KLK2 is post-ARPI mCRPC, including both PSMA-positive and PSMA-negative patients, with explicit enrollment of the ~15% of high-risk mCRPC patients who are PSMA-low/negative and currently ineligible for any PSMA-targeted therapy. Both programs are designed to operate in both pre- and post-Pluvicto settings. In the post-Pluvicto population, there is no established standard of care, and ACP3/KLK2 target different antigens with no meaningful overlap in organ-at-risk exposure beyond kidney. In the pre-Pluvicto or Pluvicto-eligible population, the intent is head-to-head superiority: ACP3 has no expression in salivary gland or kidney, eliminating Pluvicto's two dose-limiting toxicities (xerostomia and nephrotoxicity), which should enable more treatment cycles, higher cumulative tumor dose, and ultimately better PFS/OS. Against next-generation PSMA approaches such as 225Ac-PSMA-617 (Novartis PSMAcTION trial, enrolling 2025) and PNT2002 (POINT Biopharma LUNAR trial), Sego's competitive advantage is target-level, not isotope-level: PSMA RLTs, regardless of isotope, will continue to cause salivary and renal toxicity because those tissues express PSMA ([Herrmann, UroToday, 2025](https://www.urotoday.com/conference-highlights/2025-ucsf-ucla-psma-conference/159332-psma-and-beyond-2025-overview-of-clinical-trials-of-psma-rlt-in-2025.html)). ACP3 and KLK2 sidestep this entirely. The wider therapeutic index also supports earlier-line positioning, including BCR-triggered intervention in patients who have failed primary therapy but are not yet overtly metastatic, a setting where cumulative organ-at-risk tolerance becomes the binding constraint for PSMA agents.

## What is the team's view on J&J's KLK2 RLT? Aside from some of the toxicity issues that could be derived from their antibody-based format, how will Sego's KLK2 drug be positioned clinically against J&J?

JNJ-6420 validated KLK2 as a clinically tractable RLT target but demonstrated that an antibody format introduces toxicity liabilities Sego's cyclic peptide eliminates. The Phase 1 data reported four treatment-related deaths, including two from ILD at cumulative doses ≥600 μCi, with 9% ILD incidence and 63% thrombocytopenia in the ≥150 μCi cohorts ([Tagawa, JCO, 2024](https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.2024.42.16_suppl.5010)). These toxicities trace to the antibody platform, not the target: (i) prolonged blood residence (days to weeks) drives sustained marrow irradiation and cumulative lung dose, (ii) Fc-gamma receptor engagement promotes nonspecific hematologic toxicity independent of KLK2 expression, (iii) saturating mass doses amplify off-target tissue exposure. Sego's cyclic peptide eliminates all three. Short circulating half-life (~tens of minutes) reduces blood-pool radiation and lung exposure. No Fc domain removes the marrow sink. Non-saturating doses restrict binding to high-expression tumor. Phase 0 imaging will confirm lung and marrow dosimetry before any therapeutic activity is administered. Clinically, Sego will position KLK2-RLT as a backbone agent in earlier lines of prostate cancer, not dose-capped salvage therapy (J&J's current framing), where the wider therapeutic index supports more treatment cycles and higher cumulative tumor doses.

## Given that Razye's OncoACP3 binder (small molecule) is ahead of Sego in the ACP3 space, what does the team think are areas of differentiation when designing a novel peptide binder?

Sego's primary areas of differentiation are (i) designing for cellular internalization and (ii) engineering a shorter pharmacokinetic half-life to reduce bone marrow exposure. OncoACP3 binds ACP3 on the cell surface but preclinical data indicate it does not internalize, limiting the DNA damage achievable with Auger emitters (161Tb) or alpha emitters (225Ac) that are most lethal when delivered intracellularly ([Bigot, Nat Biomed Eng, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40571760/)). Sego is designing cyclic peptides that bind, internalize, and are retained in the cell, delivering the isotope where alpha and Auger emissions inflict irreparable double-strand breaks. On pharmacokinetics, the first published human dosimetry of [68Ga]Ga-OncoACP3-DOTA identified bone marrow as the highest absorbed dose organ (0.0913 mGy/MBq), above kidney (0.0657 mGy/MBq) and liver (0.0563 mGy/MBq) ([Brosch-Lenz, J Nucl Med, 2026](https://jnm.snmjournals.org/content/early/2026/04/30/jnumed.125.271933)). Our internal projections based on this dataset suggest that OncoACP3's approximately 5-hour blood half-life, when paired with 225Ac (T1/2 = 10 days), may drive cumulative marrow exposure toward dose-limiting thresholds. Cyclic peptides clear in tens of minutes, reducing blood-pool residence by an order of magnitude and proportionally reducing marrow-absorbed dose. This faster clearance, combined with internalization-driven tumor retention, is designed to widen the therapeutic index on both sides: more dose to tumor, less dose to marrow. Sego will confirm this advantage quantitatively in Phase 0 human dosimetry before advancing to therapeutic doses.

## For the targets for which ADC competitors exist (ie. CEACAM5, GCC, MUC1), how will a RLT modality for that target be differentiated? What TPP will Sego aim for, especially the safety profile?

RLT is differentiated from ADCs on these targets because its toxicity profile is predictable and quantifiable before therapeutic dosing, whereas ADC toxicity is payload- and linker-dependent, often unpredictable, and only characterized after each individual Phase 1 study. CEACAM5 ADCs (tusamitamab ravtansine) produced ocular toxicity and limited efficacy attributed to slow internalization ([Gazzah, Ann Oncol, 2022](https://www.annalsofoncology.org/article/S0923-7534%2822%2900003-5/fulltext)). MUC1-N ADCs failed on circulating decoy antigen that sequesters the antibody before it reaches tumor. TAK-164 (GCC ADC) was terminated for hepatotoxicity driven by hepatic metabolism of its maytansinoid payload via non-cleavable linker ([Kim, Cancer Chemother Pharmacol, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10068631/)). Each failure was payload- or platform-intrinsic, not target-intrinsic, yet each required a full Phase 1 to discover. ADCs also carry risk of unpredictable class toxicities like ILD that emerge independent of target biology. RLT inverts this: organ-at-risk exposure is governed by ligand PK and target expression, both measurable via Phase 0 dosimetry before any therapeutic activity is administered. Sego quantifies tumor uptake, retention, and per-organ absorbed dose in humans at sub-therapeutic levels, identifying the therapeutic index of a given molecule with dosimetric precision rather than waiting for dose-limiting toxicities to emerge clinically. Both modalities carry potential for manageable hematologic toxicity, but RLT's remaining toxicity profile (primarily renal clearance-driven kidney exposure) is fewer organs, quantifiable in advance, and addressable through medicinal chemistry optimization of clearance kinetics. This predictability is the core differentiator: Sego can characterize and optimize the safety profile of an RLT candidate before committing to therapeutic development, a structural advantage no ADC program can replicate.

## What is the accessibility of CEACAM5 and CLDN1 in normal tissues vs. the tumors in the relevant in vivo models? Do we expect any dose-limiting organs based on the normal expression profile?

CEACAM5 and CLDN1 are both expressed in normal epithelial tissues, but tumor-specific accessibility, not absolute expression, determines the relevant dose to normal organs. CEACAM5 in healthy GI epithelium is confined to the apical (luminal) surface, shielded from systemic circulation by epithelial polarity. Clinical imaging with 64Cu-M5A (an anti-CEACAM5 antibody binding the A3-B3 domain) confirmed minimal uptake in normal colon, supporting the accessibility hypothesis in humans ([Wong, Cancer Biother Radiopharm, 2023](https://www.liebertpub.com/doi/10.1089/cbr.2022.0028)). In tumors, loss of apical polarity and CEACAM5 overexpression expose the antigen to circulating ligands. CLDN1 in healthy tissues is junctional, embedded in tight junction complexes and inaccessible to circulating agents. In tumors, CLDN1 becomes non-junctional due to disrupted epithelial architecture, exposing it on the basolateral and free cell surface. The expected dose-limiting organ for both targets is kidney, driven by physiological peptide clearance rather than on-target binding. Sego's first six targets have no known red marrow expression. Medicinal chemistry optimization drives rapid renal clearance with minimal kidney retention, and Phase 0 imaging will quantify per-patient organ exposure before any therapeutic dose to confirm the preclinical accessibility model translates to humans.

## Are there any structural insights for MUC1-C that will help with the identification of a peptide binder for that specific protein region?

MUC1-C offers multiple structurally characterized binding regions that Sego plans to exploit through a dual discovery approach. The MUC1-C subunit comprises a 58-amino acid extracellular domain (ECD), a 28-amino acid transmembrane domain, and a 72-amino acid cytoplasmic domain (CD), with crystal structures available for the SEA domain at the MUC1-N/MUC1-C interface (PDB: 6BSC, [Pelaseyed, J Biol Chem, 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1570963920300029)). The cytoplasmic CQC motif (aa 87–89), a conserved CQCRRK sequence adjacent to the membrane, mediates MUC1-C homodimerization required for oncogenic signaling. GO-203, a D-amino acid cell-penetrating peptide containing the CQCRRKN sequence, validated this motif as druggable by blocking dimerization in vitro and in vivo ([Raina, PLoS ONE, 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0135156)). Sego's first approach leverages the MUC1-N/MUC1-C noncovalent heterodimeric interaction at the SEA domain to rapidly derive cyclic peptide binders that mimic the natural binding interface. In parallel, de novo screening (mRNA display, phage) targets the MUC1-C ECD directly, using commercially available constructs. The structural data on both the SEA domain cleavage interface and the CQC dimerization pocket provide defined starting points for rational peptide design, reducing the empiricism typical of de novo campaigns against novel targets.

## How will the team decide between an alpha or beta emitter for the payload for each program? What data points will drive that decision?

The choice between alpha (225Ac) and beta/Auger (161Tb) is driven by three target- and indication-specific data points: (i) disease burden distribution, (ii) internalization efficiency, and (iii) on-target/off-tumor expression profile. 225Ac is the default therapeutic isotope. Its alpha decay chain delivers four alpha particles per disintegration, producing irreparable double-strand DNA breaks with a kill density no beta emitter can match. 225Ac is preferred for targets with confirmed internalization and high receptor density, where intracellular delivery maximizes alpha-particle efficacy. 161Tb is selected when the indication presents mixed disease burden, from bulky macrometastases through micrometastases and circulating tumor cells (CTCs). Its beta emission (comparable to 177Lu) provides crossfire for debulking large lesions, while its Auger electrons deliver short-range, densely ionizing damage to single CTCs and sub-millimeter micromets. 161Tb may also be preferred when the on-target/off-tumor expression profile of a given target favors more forgiving dosimetry. The decision is made empirically at the program level using Phase 0 human dosimetry data: tumor uptake and retention, organ-at-risk washout kinetics, and predicted absorbed dose ratios under each isotope's decay scheme. A single DOTA/DOTAGA chelator is compatible with both isotopes, so the decision can be deferred to clinical data without requiring separate medicinal chemistry campaigns.

## Why the usage of 161Tb and why is it preferred over 177Lu? What is the current CMC process – please explain the raw materials, nuclear reaction, and decay process. Who are the current manufacturers of 161Tb and how much supply can they support?

161Tb delivers approximately twice the per-GBq tumor absorbed dose of 177Lu in head-to-head PSMA dosimetry studies, driven by its additional high-energy Auger and conversion electron emissions that 177Lu lacks ([Liang, Am J Nucl Med Mol Imaging, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12629902/); [Hofman, Lancet Oncol, 2025](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanonc/article/PIIS1470-2045(25)00332-8/abstract)). Both isotopes have comparable beta emissions, but 161Tb's Auger electrons deposit intense radiation at sub-cellular range (nanometers), making it effective against micrometastases and CTCs where beta crossfire is wasted. Early clinical data demonstrate responses in patients who progressed after standard 177Lu therapy, confirming the added therapeutic value. The CMC process starts with highly enriched 160Gd (>98% isotopic purity versus ~21.9% natural abundance) as the target material. 160Gd undergoes neutron capture in a nuclear reactor (160Gd + n → 161Gd), and the resulting 161Gd decays by electron capture with a 3.7-minute half-life to 161Tb (T1/2 = 6.89 days). This production pathway is nearly identical to 177Lu manufacturing, which is well established commercially. Current cGMP manufacturers include TerThera B.V. (Netherlands), ITM Isotope Technologies Munich (Germany), and Isotopia Molecular Imaging (Israel), with Nusano (Salt Lake City, online 2027) as an additional strategic supplier for Sego. Supply has expanded substantially in the past 12 months, with multiple suppliers pursuing US DMF filings and draft USP/Ph.Eur. monographs under development. Current combined capacity is sufficient for clinical trial supply across the field, and production is expected to scale congruently with clinical development timelines through 2028–2029.

## Where is Sego planning to source their supply of 225Ac and 161Tb? Any expected impacts to the supply chain in the future?

Sego's isotope supply chain is anchored by the strategic relationship with Nusano, an isotope production facility adjacent to Sego's building in Salt Lake City, which will supply both 225Ac and 161Tb when operational in 2027. For 225Ac prior to Nusano's commissioning, Sego has identified multiple qualified suppliers: Eckert & Ziegler (Germany, R&D and cGMP), Actineer/ITM (Germany/Canada, cGMP), and NorthStar Medical Radioisotopes (US, R&D scale), with additional capacity from TerraPower Isotopes (US) and PanTera (EU). For 161Tb, TerThera B.V. (Netherlands), ITM Munich (Germany), and Isotopia (Israel) all provide cGMP material today. The University of Utah, 23 minutes from Sego's facility, has published extensively on 161Tb production and supplies research quantities from its TRIGA reactor. The primary supply chain risk for 225Ac remains production method maturity: most current supply derives from 229Th generators or proton irradiation of 226Ra, both of which are scaling but not yet at commercial volumes. Nusano's linear accelerator-based production method offers a domestic, non-reactor alternative that diversifies this risk. For 161Tb, the bottleneck is enriched 160Gd starting material, historically concentrated among Russian suppliers (Rosatom). Western investment in domestic enrichment capacity (DOE Isotope Program, URENCO) is actively mitigating this geopolitical exposure. Sego's proximity to Nusano minimizes isotope transit time and decay losses, a structural logistics advantage that compounds across all six programs.

Stage 1 of the CMC build (designated Stage 1A) is a GMP-qualified radiopharmaceutical manufacturing suite designed to supply all clinical material from Phase 0 through Phase 2 studies. Construction is already in flight, with completion targeted for December 2026 to be operational before the first clinical manufacturing need in 2028. Ongoing operational overhead for the facility runs approximately $1.2M per year. The Stage 1A suite includes hot laboratories for radiolabeling, quality control, and release testing, with shared radiation shielding, HVAC, and waste handling infrastructure that also serves preclinical radiochemistry needs. Internal production costs for Phase 0 programs are estimated at $1.4M versus $3M at a CDMO, with savings compounding across each additional program.

Stage 2 of the CMC build, which adds expanded capacity to support Phase 3 and commercial supply, is already planned for deferral to the Series A and could alternatively be outsourced to a CDMO. Stage 1A is not trancheable without undermining the core economic rationale: deferring the build to Series A (mid-2028) would mean the facility is not operational until late 2029, after the first two Phase 0 programs have already run through CDMO at approximately 2x the internal production cost. The capacity is not overbuilt for a single program; it is right-sized for the planned portfolio cadence of 2 Phase 0 programs in 2028 and 2 additional in 2029, with Phase 1 manufacturing overlapping. Importantly, the shared infrastructure (shielding, HVAC, waste handling, personnel) serves preclinical hot laboratory needs in parallel, amortizing the capital across all programs and de-risking the CRO dependency for in vivo hot work.
